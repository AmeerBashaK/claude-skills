# Pop art: history and artists to borrow from

Pop art emerged in mid-1950s Britain and late-1950s/1960s America. It took its subjects from mass culture (comic strips, advertising, billboards, TV, packaging, celebrity) and often copied the methods of mass reproduction. Compared with Abstract Expressionism it was light, humorous and accessible, and it removed the artist's hand, questioning originality.

| Artist | What to borrow for motion |
|---|---|
| Roy Lichtenstein | Comic panels blown up: Ben-Day dots (named after printer Benjamin Henry Day), flat primaries, thick outlines, hard edges, balloons and SFX ("Whaam!", "Drowning Girl"). Strong emotion (love, war) shown deadpan. His "industrial texture": hand-made imitation of mechanical print. |
| Andy Warhol | Silkscreen: ink pressed through a stencilled mesh. Serial repetition of one image in different colours (Marilyn, Campbell's Soup, Coca-Cola), misregistered plates, celebrity and consumer goods. |
| Richard Hamilton | British collage ("Just what is it that makes today's homes so different, so appealing?", 1956): cut-out ads and products collaged into a scene. |
| Peter Blake | Collage, badges, celebrity cut-outs (Sgt. Pepper cover). |
| James Rosenquist | Billboard-scale fragments of ads overlapping, extreme crops. |
| Tom Wesselmann | Flat colour still lifes and figures, very clean edges, consumer objects. |
| Claes Oldenburg | Everyday objects made giant and soft; scale play. |
| Robert Indiana | Type as image (LOVE): bold stacked letters in flat colour. |
| Keith Haring (later) | Bold line figures, motion marks around figures, flat colour. Great for loopable line animation. |
| Contemporary | Neo-pop sticker style, Takashi Murakami superflat, comic-style brand reels, Spider-Verse halftone and misregistration in animation. |

Print techniques to imitate: Ben-Day dots (even dots), halftone (variable dots), silkscreen (flat plates, misregistration), offset/CMYK (overlapping dot screens at different angles).
